But there are several different ways to make that bet, and the economics behind currencies are more complicated than simply asking "will this country get richer?"

There are several quite different things people call "currency investing":

| Investment | Main source of return | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Foreign cash | Currency appreciation + interest | Currency depreciation |
| Foreign bank deposit | Interest + currency movement | Currency + bank risk |
| Foreign government bonds | Bond yield + currency movement | Interest rates + currency |
| Foreign corporate bonds | Yield + currency movement | Credit + currency |
| Currency ETF | Currency movement ± yield | Currency + fund structure |
| FX trading | Currency movement | Leverage/volatility |
| Foreign stocks | Company growth + currency movement | Equity + currency |
| Foreign real estate | Property return + currency movement | Property + currency |

```

 ↓
JPY
 ↓
JPY appreciates against AUD
 ↓
profit when converted back to AUD
```

```
AUD
 ↓
JPY
 ↓
Japanese company
 ↓
company return
 +
JPY/AUD movement
 ↓
AUD return
```

You therefore have **two investment engines**.

## 2\. The first thing I'd look at: exchange-rate regime

This is exactly where your UAE example becomes relevant.

Currencies generally fall into categories such as:

```
Exchange-rate regimes
                  │
   ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
   │              │              │
Floating       Managed         Pegged
   │              │              │
 AUD            CNY             AED
 GBP            SGD             HKD*
 JPY            etc.            etc.
```

\*The exact regime and strength of a peg still need to be examined individually.

### Floating currency

The market largely determines its exchange rate.

Examples include the:

- AUD
- USD
- EUR
- GBP
- JPY

These can move substantially because of interest rates, economic conditions, capital flows, commodity prices, risk sentiment, etc.

### Pegged currency

The central bank attempts to maintain a particular exchange rate.

The UAE dirham is a particularly clear example. The CBUAE explicitly maintains the AED/USD peg and intervenes in the FX market to maintain it. Its published intervention rates are around **USD/AED 3.672–3.673**. [^1]

So your reasoning is basically correct:

> **If AED is pegged to USD, buying AED instead of USD generally isn't giving you a separate currency thesis.**

From an AUD perspective, both are effectively giving you exposure to the USD/AUD relationship.

There is one important caveat:

### A peg is not the same thing as a law of nature.

You need to consider:

- foreign-exchange reserves
- government finances
- current-account position
- political stability
- credibility of the central bank
- ability to defend the peg
- external debt
- capital flows

A peg can theoretically be abandoned, devalued or adjusted.

## 3\. Interest-rate differential

This is one of the biggest factors in currency markets.

Suppose:

```
Australia interest rate:     4%
Country X interest rate:     9%
```

At first glance, Country X looks attractive.

But why is its interest rate 9%?

It could be because:

```
Inflation = 7%
Real interest = ~2%
```

while Australia might have:

```
Inflation = 2.5%
Interest = 4%
Real interest = ~1.5%
```

The headline interest rate alone therefore tells you very little.

### Look at:

**Nominal interest rate − inflation ≈ real interest rate**

And also look at the **expected future path** of interest rates.

Currency markets care heavily about:

> "What will interest rates be relative to expectations?"

rather than simply:

> "What is the rate today?"

---

## 4\. Inflation

This is probably the most fundamental long-term currency consideration.

If a country continuously experiences substantially higher inflation than its trading partners, its currency can lose purchasing power.

For example:

```
Country A
Inflation: 2%

Country B
Inflation: 15%
```

If that persists for years, you'd want a very good reason to hold Country B's currency.

High inflation can:

- reduce domestic purchasing power
- reduce investor confidence
- encourage capital flight
- cause central-bank tightening
- eventually destabilize the currency

But **high inflation does not automatically mean a currency will fall tomorrow**.

Currency markets price expectations.

## 5\. Central bank policy

You should know what the country's central bank is trying to accomplish.

Look at:

- inflation target
- policy interest rate
- rate trajectory
- quantitative easing/tightening
- FX intervention
- currency targets
- reserve policy
- willingness to tolerate inflation
- independence from government

For a floating currency:

```
Inflation ↑
     ↓
Central bank may raise rates
     ↓
Currency may become more attractive
```

But this isn't guaranteed.

And for a pegged currency, monetary policy can be constrained by the currency it is pegged to. The IMF notes that countries with currency pegs generally have interest rates that closely track those of the anchor currency. [^2]

## 6\. Government debt and fiscal position

This is particularly important for currencies of countries with large debts.

Look at:

### Government debt

```
Debt / GDP
```

but don't stop there.

Also examine:

- interest payments / government revenue
- maturity structure
- foreign-currency debt
- fiscal deficit
- ability to raise taxes
- government spending
- debt held domestically vs externally

A country with:

```
Debt/GDP = 100%
```

isn't necessarily worse than one with:

```
Debt/GDP = 50%
```

because the composition matters enormously.

For example:

```
Country A
100% debt/GDP
mostly domestic
low interest rates
long maturities
strong tax base

Country B
50% debt/GDP
large foreign-currency debt
high interest rates
short maturities
weak tax base
```

Country B could have substantially greater currency vulnerability.

## 7\. Current account / trade balance

This is an important one that is often overlooked.

Look at:

```
Exports
  −
Imports
  =
Trade balance
```

and more broadly:

```
Current account
+
Capital/financial flows
≈
Balance of payments
```

A country that consistently imports substantially more than it exports needs foreign capital to finance the difference.

That isn't necessarily bad.

But you want to know:

> **Where is the foreign currency coming from?**

Compare:

### Country A

```
Large trade surplus
Strong exports
Large FX reserves
Low external debt
```

versus:

### Country B

```
Large current-account deficit
High external borrowing
Low reserves
Heavy reliance on foreign capital
```

The second situation can make a currency more vulnerable if investors suddenly withdraw capital.

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓

      ↓

      ↓
Trade balance improves
      ↓
Economic outlook improves
      ↓

The reverse can also happen.

## 9\. Political stability and institutions

You don't necessarily need a politically "perfect" country.

What matters financially is things such as:

- property-rights security
- rule of law
- central-bank credibility
- government stability
- corruption
- capital controls
- ability to move money across borders
- likelihood of sudden regulatory changes
- treatment of foreign investors

This becomes especially important in emerging markets.

A currency can look extremely cheap according to economic metrics and remain cheap because investors don't trust the institutional environment.

## 10\. Capital controls

This is a **big one**.

You need to ask:

> "Can I actually get my money out?"

Some countries restrict:

- converting local currency into foreign currency
- transferring capital overseas
- purchasing foreign assets
- withdrawing large amounts of foreign currency

Therefore:

```
Cheap currency
      ≠
Good investment
```

if you cannot freely move the money.

## 11\. Currency reserves

For countries managing or defending their currency, foreign-exchange reserves matter.

Consider:

```
FX reserves
      ↓
Ability to defend currency
      ↓
Confidence in exchange-rate regime
```

This is particularly important for pegged currencies.

The UAE, for example, explicitly maintains foreign reserves to support its fixed exchange-rate regime. [^3]

## 12\. Currency valuation

Now we get into something particularly interesting for an investor:

> **Is the currency expensive or cheap relative to its economic fundamentals?**

There are several ways to investigate this.

### Purchasing Power Parity — PPP

Very simplified:

```
Price of goods in Country A
─────────────────────────────
Price of equivalent goods in Country B
```

can provide an indication of relative purchasing power.

But PPP is **not a good short-term trading signal**.

Currencies can remain overvalued or undervalued for years.

---

## 13\. Real effective exchange rate — REER

This is more sophisticated.

Instead of asking:

> AUD/JPY?

you examine a currency against a **basket of trading partners**, adjusted for inflation.

Conceptually:

```
Currency value
      +
Trading partners
      +
Trade weights
      +
Relative inflation
      ↓
REER
```

This can give you a much better picture of whether a currency is historically expensive or cheap.

## 14\. Economic growth

GDP growth matters, but don't make the mistake of thinking:

> "Country grows faster → currency automatically rises."

Not necessarily.

Imagine:

```
Country X

GDP growth       +8%
Inflation        +12%
Current deficit  large
Debt             rising rapidly
Capital flight   increasing
```

High GDP growth isn't enough.

You want to understand **why** the economy is growing and whether the growth is sustainable.

## 15\. Population and demographics

This is more of a **long-term structural factor**.

Consider:

- population growth
- working-age population
- dependency ratio
- productivity
- migration
- ageing
- birth rates

For example:

```
Working-age population ↑
        +
Productivity ↑
        +
Investment ↑
        ↓
Potential economic expansion
```

Whereas:

```
Working-age population ↓
        +
Ageing population ↑
        ↓
Potential fiscal/productivity pressures
```

This isn't a direct currency signal, but it matters over decades.

## 16\. External debt — especially foreign-currency debt

This is one of the more important risk indicators.

Suppose a country earns money in its own currency but owes billions in USD.

If its currency collapses:

```
Local currency ↓ 30%

USD debt
       ↓
becomes ~43% more expensive
in local-currency terms
```

That can create a vicious cycle:

```
Currency falls
    ↓
Foreign debt becomes harder to service
    ↓
Financial stress
    ↓
Capital leaves
    ↓
Currency falls further
```

This is one reason emerging-market currencies can behave very differently from USD/EUR/GBP/JPY/AUD.

## 17\. Liquidity

Don't underestimate this.

There is a huge difference between:

```
USD
EUR
JPY
GBP
```

and a relatively obscure emerging-market currency.

With an illiquid currency you may encounter:

- wider spreads
- poorer execution
- fewer financial products
- higher transaction costs
- difficulty exiting
- greater price gaps

So even if your fundamental thesis is correct, you can lose money through implementation.

## 18\. Your own currency matters

This is probably the biggest conceptual mistake people make.

Don't ask:

> "Will USD go up?"

Ask:

> **"Will USD go up relative to AUD?"**

Everything is relative.

For you:

```
USD/AUD
EUR/AUD
JPY/AUD
GBP/AUD
CHF/AUD
```

are the relevant relationships.

A currency can be falling against USD while simultaneously rising against AUD.

## 19\. Correlation is important

You don't necessarily want five currencies that all behave the same way.

For example:

```
Portfolio

USD   ─┐
CHF   ─┼── defensive / similar behaviour
JPY   ─┘

AUD   ─┐
NZD   ─┼── commodity/risk-sensitive exposure
CAD   ─┘
```

The exact correlations change over time, but the principle is useful.

You want to know:

> **What economic factor am I actually diversifying into?**

rather than merely counting currencies.

## 20\. Carry trade

This is one of the major currency strategies.

Suppose:

```
Borrow/hold currency A: 3%
Invest currency B:     8%

Interest differential: +5%
```

You might think:

> "Easy 5%."

It isn't.

If currency B falls 10% against currency A:

```
+5% interest
-10% FX movement
----------------
≈ -5%
```

before transaction costs and taxes.

That's the central danger of carry.

A high-yield currency often has a high yield **for a reason**.

## 21\. Currency intervention

Some governments/central banks actively influence their currency.

They may:

- buy their own currency
- sell their own currency
- accumulate foreign reserves
- impose capital controls
- establish trading bands
- peg the currency

This changes the investment thesis considerably.

The IMF notes that exchange-rate intervention is used by many countries with managed or pegged currencies, and that intervention can involve trade-offs and costs. [^2]

## 22\. Geopolitical risk

Consider:

- sanctions
- wars
- trade restrictions
- diplomatic conflicts
- shipping routes
- energy supply
- strategic commodities
- foreign investment restrictions

A currency doesn't exist in isolation from geopolitics.

## 24\. Transaction costs

Currency investing can have deceptively high friction.

Your actual return can look like:

```
Currency appreciation
+ interest
- FX spread
- broker/platform fees
- conversion costs
- account fees
- taxes
= actual return
```

If you're moving relatively small amounts, the spread can become significant.

## 2\. Inflation becomes more important than the headline interest rate

Because you're holding **cash**, you don't receive the country's interest rate.

So:

> **A 10% interest rate doesn't give you 10% return.**

You get:

```
Currency appreciation/depreciation
```

only.

However, interest rates still matter indirectly because they influence capital flows and expectations.

For example:

```
Currency A
Inflation: 2%
Interest: 4%

Currency B
Inflation: 15%
Interest: 18%
```

Currency B's 18% interest rate doesn't help you because you're holding physical banknotes.

And its:

```
18% nominal interest
− 15% inflation
≈ 3% real interest
```

suggests that the enormous interest rate may largely be compensating for enormous inflation.

For physical currency, **persistent low inflation is generally a much more attractive characteristic** than simply having a high nominal interest rate.

## 1\. How a currency peg actually works

Take the UAE dirham.

The UAE effectively says:

> **We want the AED/USD exchange rate to remain around a particular level.**

The CBUAE maintains the dirham at about **AED 3.672–3.673 per USD**. This isn't simply a promise written on paper—the central bank uses its monetary/FX tools and reserves to make the market exchange rate stay close to the target. [^4]

Think of it like this:

```
UAE CENTRAL BANK
                    │
                    │ target
                    ▼
             ~3.672 AED/USD
                    │
      ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
      │                           │
Market wants AED ↑          Market wants AED ↓
      │                           │
AED becomes too strong      AED becomes too weak
      │                           │
Central bank can            Central bank can
sell AED / buy USD          sell USD / buy AED
      │                           │
      └─────────────┬─────────────┘
                    ▼
            Push exchange rate
              toward the peg
```

The exact operational mechanics depend on the country's arrangement, but the fundamental concept is:

> **The central bank uses its balance sheet and monetary/FX tools to make the currency trade near the chosen exchange rate.**

## Why does this require reserves?

Imagine everyone suddenly wants USD instead of AED.

They sell:

```
AED → USD
```

That creates downward pressure on AED.

The central bank can use its USD reserves to buy AED:

```
Central bank:

USD reserves ↓
AED holdings/market demand ↑
```

which supports the AED.

That is why foreign reserves matter enormously for countries defending pegs.

The IMF defines reserve assets as external assets controlled by monetary authorities that can be used for balance-of-payments needs and foreign-exchange intervention. [^5]

## 2\. Why does a country choose a peg?

There are several reasons.

### Reason 1 — Stability

Suppose a country conducts enormous amounts of trade with the US.

A stable USD relationship makes:

```

exports
contracts
pricing
investment
```

more predictable.

### Reason 2 — Reduce currency volatility

A floating currency might move:

```
1.00
↓
0.93
↓
1.08
↓
0.97
```

A peg attempts to suppress that movement.

### Reason 3 — Inflation credibility

A country with a history of monetary instability may effectively say:

> "We will tie our monetary system to a currency that markets already trust."

### Reason 4 — Trade/investment attractiveness

Businesses may prefer operating in a country where exchange-rate risk is lower.

## 4\. Inflation — the fundamental concept

Inflation means:

> **The general price level of goods and services is increasing over time.**

If a basket of goods costs:

```
Year 1: $100
Year 2: $105
```

then the basket experienced approximately:

**5% inflation.**

The important distinction is:

### Individual price increase ≠ necessarily inflation

If oranges go:

```
$3 → $5
```

that's an increase in the price of oranges.

Inflation refers to a **broad increase in prices across the economy**.

## 5\. Inflation terminology

Here's the terminology I'd recommend learning.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inflation** | General increase in prices | CPI rises 5% |
| **Deflation** | General decrease in prices | CPI falls 2% |
| **Disinflation** | Inflation is still positive but slowing | 8% → 5% → 3% |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely rapid inflation | Prices rising extraordinarily quickly |
| **CPI** | Consumer Price Index; measures prices of a consumer basket | CPI = 120 vs base 100 |
| **Core inflation** | Inflation measure excluding particularly volatile components | CPI 4%, core 3.5% |
| **Headline inflation** | Broad published inflation measure | CPI = 4% |
| **Inflation rate** | Percentage change in the price index | CPI 100 → 105 = 5% |
| **Purchasing power** | How much goods/services a unit of currency can buy | A$100 buys less after inflation |
| **Real value** | Value after accounting for inflation | $100 today vs $100 five years ago |
| **Nominal** | Value measured in current monetary units | Nominal GDP |
| **Real** | Adjusted for inflation | Real GDP |
| **Inflation expectation** | What households/businesses/markets expect future inflation to be | Expected inflation = 3% |
| **Imported inflation** | Domestic prices rise because imports become more expensive | Currency falls → imported fuel rises |
| **Demand-pull inflation** | Demand exceeds productive capacity | Consumers spend heavily while supply is constrained |
| **Cost-push inflation** | Production costs rise | Energy/wages/raw materials become more expensive |
| **Wage-price spiral** | Wages and prices reinforce one another | Prices ↑ → wages ↑ → costs ↑ → prices ↑ |
| **Monetary inflation** | Inflation associated with excessive monetary expansion relative to economic output | Money/credit grows substantially faster than output |
| **Base effect** | Current inflation rate is affected by unusually high/low prices a year earlier | A previous oil spike distorts today's annual rate |

## 25\. Government debt, fiscal deficit, debt servicing, foreign debt

Here's the table you asked for.

| Metric | What it means | Why it matters to currency | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Government debt** | Total accumulated government borrowing | High/rising debt can eventually undermine fiscal credibility | Government owes $1T |
| **Fiscal deficit** | Government spends more than it receives during a period | Persistent deficits increase debt unless offset elsewhere | Revenue $500B, spending $550B → $50B deficit |
| **Fiscal surplus** | Government receives more than it spends | Can reduce debt or borrowing requirements | Revenue $550B, spending $500B → $50B surplus |

## 32\. Your purchasing-power question

You asked:

> "so currencies with strong purchasing power can be a good investment regardless of strength comparing to aud? so i just look into political and economic factors to better decide?"

**Almost, but there's an important distinction.**

There are three separate concepts:

```

                 │
       ┌─────────┼─────────┐
       ↓         ↓         ↓
Purchasing    AUD FX     Economic/
  power       value      political
       │         │         │
       │         │         │
What can it   What can   Why might

              Australia? change?
```

### Purchasing power

Tells you:

> How much can this currency buy domestically?

### AUD exchange rate

Tells you:

> How many AUD will this currency ultimately be worth?

### Economic/political fundamentals

Help you estimate:

> What could cause that exchange rate to change?

## 39\. Another important distinction: level vs trend

Don't just record:

```

Record:

  2% → 2.5% → 3% → 4%

                accelerating

Debt/GDP
  60 → 63 → 67 → 70
                  ↑
               worsening

Reserves
  90 → 95 → 100 → 110
                    ↑
                 improving
```

**Direction can be more informative than the absolute number.**
